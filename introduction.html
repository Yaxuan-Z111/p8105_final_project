Introduction

Background

World Health Organization (WHO), in the 1970s, first introduced the term Non-Insulin Dependent Diabetes Mellitus (NIDDM) to distinguish forms of diabetes based on therapeutic requirements and underlying mechanisms. This classification separated insulin-dependent diabetes (now classified as Type 1) from cases typically managed without insulin. As scientific understanding progressed, it became evident that NIDDM involved pathophysiological features such as insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction, prompting the 1990s shift toward the term Type 2 Diabetes (T2D)—a terminology that more accurately reflects biological mechanisms rather than treatment modalities [1].

T2D is now recognized as a complex, multifactorial chronic disease, accounting for more than 90% of all diabetes cases globally. Its prevalence is projected to rise dramatically, from 460 million in 2017 to 693 million by 2045 [2], with the greatest burdens falling on low- and middle-income countries [1]. Saeedi et al. [4] estimated a global diabetes prevalence of 9.3% in 2019, expected to increase to 10.9% by 2045, revealing substantial disparities between urban and rural populations. Although genetic predisposition to T2D is well-established [3], increasing evidence highlights the critical influence of postnatal, modifiable exposures, and how their synergistic or interacting effects shape disease onset. For instance, Mendelian randomization research suggests that education may directly reduce T2D risk independent of BMI [5], though biological mechanisms remain insufficiently understood.

Given this complex interplay of biological, behavioral, psychological, and environmental factors, there remain major gaps in quantifying how these determinants collectively contribute to T2D risk. This study aims to investigate these under-explored domains and provide insights that can guide precision prevention strategies.

Risky Factors

A substantial body of literature demonstrates that unhealthy lifestyle factors—smoking, low educational attainment, poor mental health, and physical inactivity—significantly contribute to the development and progression of T2D [6]. Understanding both single-factor and cumulative effects is crucial, as these dimensions frequently co-occur and reinforce one another.

Lifestyle and Behavioral Factors

The rising global T2D prevalence is closely associated with the spread of unhealthy lifestyle patterns. Importantly, lifestyle interventions such as weight reduction and increased physical activity have demonstrated substantial benefits, especially among individuals with a familial predisposition to T2D [7].

Smoking is a particularly strong behavioral risk factor. Tobacco exposure promotes insulin resistance and systemic inflammation, key mechanisms underlying T2D [8]. Willi et al. [9] reported that active smokers exhibit a 44% increased risk, which escalates to 61% in heavy smokers, indicating a clear dose–response pattern.

Psychosocial Determinants

Mental health also plays a meaningful role. Conditions such as depression, anxiety, and chronic stress are associated with neuroendocrine disruptions, inflammatory activation, and unhealthy coping behaviors—all of which increase T2D risk. Elevated anxiety is associated with higher HbA1c and blood glucose levels [10], while Herder (2017) emphasized that chronic stress exacerbates insulin resistance and impairs glycemic regulation [11].

Physical Inactivity and Mobility

Physical inactivity represents another major contributor. Dejong (2019) found that sedentary individuals have a 30% increased risk of developing T2D [12]. Travel patterns may further influence metabolic health by disrupting routines, lowering physical activity, and worsening dietary habits [13]. Conversely, active travel and personalized lifestyle interventions have shown promise in reducing T2D incidence and modifying genetic susceptibility [14].

Socioeconomic Status and Education

Educational attainment is a powerful structural determinant of health. Higher education is associated with improved health literacy, preventive behaviors, and access to healthcare services [15]. Lower education levels correspond with higher rates of chronic diseases, including T2D.

Medical Comorbidities and Health Status

Beyond lifestyle and psychosocial variables, several comorbid conditions included in the NHIS and in our model—such as hypertension, high cholesterol, liver disease, stroke, coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction, and depression—reflect biological pathways involving inflammation, vascular dysfunction, lipid abnormalities, and chronic systemic stress. These cardiometabolic and psychosocial factors are known to independently increase T2D risk and may also interact with lifestyle and socioeconomic determinants.

General health status and mobility difficulties reflect underlying physiological functioning. Functional limitations can reduce physical activity, reinforce sedentary behavior, and exacerbate metabolic dysfunction, contributing further to T2D development.Physical inactivity is another major contributor. A longitudinal study by Dejong (2019) found that sedentary individuals had a 30% higher risk of developing T2D [12]. Frequent travel, often associated with disrupted routines, irregular eating patterns, and reduced physical activity, may further exacerbate these risks by promoting sedentary behavior and unhealthy habits [13]. However, recent evidence suggests that promoting active travel behaviors can significantly improve physical activity levels and reduce the risk of chronic diseases, including T2D. Furthermore, studies have indicated that addressing lifestyle factors like diet and physical activity may prevent the progression of prediabetes to full T2D, and that even genetic susceptibility to T2D may be modifiable through lifestyle changes, with personalized interventions showing promise. Personalized interventions targeting lifestyle changes, such as increased physical activity and dietary modifications, have shown promise in preventing and managing T2D [14].

By incorporating this broader constellation of behavioral, psychosocial, socioeconomic, and chronic disease variables, our study aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of T2D risk in the U.S. adult population. The integration of these diverse NHIS measures allows for a nuanced evaluation of how complex risk pathways intersect to influence diabetes susceptibility.

Current Gaps

Despite substantial scientific progress, major gaps persist in understanding the interaction effects among lifestyle, socioeconomic status, mental health, and chronic diseases in shaping T2D risk. While Yu et al. [16] highlight diet and pollution as emerging risk factors—potentially mediated through epigenetic mechanisms—these pathways remain insufficiently characterized.

Similarly, although the EPIC-InterAct study demonstrated that individuals with lower education have a 67% increased risk even after adjusting for lifestyle and metabolic factors [17], the mechanisms beyond behavioral changes remain unclear.

Additionally, much existing literature prioritizes prenatal and genetic determinants of T2D [2,11], whereas postnatal behavioral, psychosocial, and socioeconomic exposures are comparatively understudied. Travel behavior remains controversial, with inconsistent evidence regarding its role in metabolic health. Moreover, population disparities in T2D burden demonstrate that generalized prevention strategies remain insufficient [18].

Thus, integrative research is urgently needed to clarify how these factors jointly shape diabetes susceptibility.

Research Objective

To address these gaps, this study systematically investigates how modifiable behavioral factors (smoking, mobility limitations, physical inactivity), psychosocial factors (depression), socioeconomic status (education), general health status, and cardiometabolic comorbidities (hypertension, high cholesterol, liver disease, coronary heart disease, angina, myocardial infarction) interact to shape the risk of developing T2D.

By leveraging NHIS data and modeling variables including SEX_A, AGEP_A, BMI_A, DIFF_A, EDUCP_A, PHSTAT_A, CHLEV_A, HYPEV_A, LIVEREV_A, MIEV_A, CHDEV_A, DEPEV_A, this research aims to construct a comprehensive profile of T2D risk rooted in biological, psychosocial, and socioeconomic determinants. The findings will support the development of more precise, equitable, and context-sensitive public health prevention strategies to reduce the growing burden of T2D.
