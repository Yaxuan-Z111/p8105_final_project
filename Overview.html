Multivariable Logistic Analysis of Type II Diabetes Risk Considering Demographic, Mental Health and Lifestyles

Overview

This project analyzed 2024 NHIS adult data to identify key factors associated with Type 2 diabetes using multivariable logistic regression. We integrated demographic, BMI, functional status, education, general health, and cardiometabolic comorbidities, checked correlation/confounding, refined predictors, and selected a parsimonious model using BIC, with results summarized in a report, website, and brief screencast.

The link to the repository of this project can be found here.

Type II Diabetes

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a major global public health problem. In the United States alone, more than 38 million people are living with diabetes, and an estimated 90%–95% of these cases are type 2 diabetes. As a chronic condition, T2D is associated with substantial morbidity and an average reduction in life expectancy of around ten years. At the same time, T2D can often be prevented or delayed through evidence-based lifestyle changes, including weight management, healthy eating, and regular physical activity. (CDC, 2024)

Data Source

We use data from the 2024 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), a nationally representative, cross-sectional survey of the civilian noninstitutionalized U.S. population conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). T2D status, anxiety, depression, smoking, and relevant demographic and clinical covariates are derived from the publicly available NHIS sample adult-level files.

Learn more about the data here.

Statistical Analysis

Logistic regression models were fit with diabetes status (DIBEV_A) as the binary outcome, incorporating selected demographic, BMI, functional, socioeconomic, mental health, and cardiometabolic predictors. To address multicollinearity, correlation screening identified the strong height–weight relationship, so these measures were replaced with a continuous BMI variable. Age, sex, BMI, and education were prespecified as core confounders; their impact was evaluated by comparing models with and without adjustment and examining changes in odds ratios. Model fit and parsimony were then assessed using AIC/BIC, and the reduced significant-term model was selected for reporting.

T2D Prevalence Visualization

The New York State T2D Map

df_raw = read_csv(
  "DiabetesAtlas_CountyData.csv",
  skip = 2
)

ny_df = df_raw |> 
  filter(State == "New York") |> 
  mutate(
    CountyFIPS = sprintf("%05d", as.numeric(CountyFIPS)),
    Percentage = as.numeric(Percentage),
    hover = paste0(
      "County: ", County, 
      "<br>Percentage: ", Percentage, "%"
    )
  )

geo_url = "https://raw.githubusercontent.com/plotly/datasets/master/geojson-counties-fips.json"

fig = plot_ly(
  data = ny_df,
  type = "choropleth",
  geojson = geo_url,
  locations = ~CountyFIPS,
  z = ~Percentage,
  colorscale = "Blues",
  text = ~hover,
  hoverinfo = "text",
  marker = list(line = list(width = 0))
) |> 
  layout(
    title = "Prevalence of T2D by County (New York State)",
    geo = list(
      fitbounds = "locations",
      visible = FALSE
    )
  )

Across New York State, the age-adjusted prevalence of diagnosed diabetes in 2023 shows substantial variation across counties, ranging from approximately 7% to over 12%. Many rural counties—including Allegany, Cattaraugus, Essex, Franklin, Montgomery, Niagara, Steuben, and Schuyler—report some of the highest diabetes percentages, often exceeding 10%, with upper confidence limits reaching 13–14%. In contrast, several counties such as Dutchess, Tompkins, and Putnam show comparatively lower estimates, generally around 7–8%. Urban counties also demonstrate variability: Bronx County continues to exhibit one of the highest burdens at 12.1%, while New York County (Manhattan) reports a lower estimate of 6.5%, the lowest in the state. The distribution suggests a complex interplay of demographic, socioeconomic, and healthcare access factors contributing to geographic disparities in diabetes prevalence across New York. These data highlight the need for targeted public health strategies that account for regional differences in risk and resource availability.
